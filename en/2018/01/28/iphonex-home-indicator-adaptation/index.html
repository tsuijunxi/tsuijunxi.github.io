iPhone X Home Indicator Adaptation

Posted on 2018-01-28 | In iOS
Words count in article: 19k | Reading time ≈ 1:09
中文 / English

Preface

Apple has always liked doing things in its own way, and the arrival of iPhone X was even more surprising. Apple fans were naturally excited, but because of the notch, iOS engineers had to deal with a rather painful adaptation process.

There are already many articles about iPhone X adaptation online, including some excellent ones. I once handled the iPhone X adaptation work for QQReader by myself, but many solutions to common adaptation issues are similar. This article will not repeat those well-known problems. Instead, it focuses on the iPhone X Home Indicator, a seemingly minor adaptation point that is easy to forget.

Where the Problem Starts

For most apps, adapting the Home Indicator may not be necessary at all, because the home bar constantly reminds users to change their old Home button habits. Its purpose is to replace the original Home button.

However, for certain apps, such as reading apps, video apps, and games, the core experience is immersive. In those scenarios, the Home Indicator becomes especially distracting, particularly the white glow in night mode.

You can feel it first. The screenshots below are from the simulator; on a real device, the experience is even more uncomfortable.

I roughly checked several reading apps, including iReader, WeChat Reading, Baidu Reading, and Shuqi Novel. Basically none of them adapted this behavior. Maybe the iPhone X market share was still small at the time. But from the current perspective, adapting the Home Indicator is still necessary. Next, let’s use QQReader as an example to analyze the adaptation process.

Initial Adaptation in Day Mode

Following the official guidance, let’s start adapting.

First look at the UIViewController header. iOS 11 added a UIHomeIndicatorAutoHidden category to UIViewController, used to control automatic hiding of the home indicator.

The control mechanism for HomeIndicator is actually quite similar to status-bar control. So adaptation starts with the APIs above.

    1. Override prefersHomeIndicatorAutoHidden in the view controller where you want to hide the indicator.
    1. Call setNeedsUpdateOfHomeIndicatorAutoHidden at the appropriate time.

Is that enough? Apparently not. The code above may not take effect. The reason is:

Overriding this method on a controller only applies when the controller has no parent controller. Most apps use UITabBarController or UINavigationController as the root controller of the window, with UIViewController used to present content. In that case, the root controller’s childViewControllerForHomeIndicatorAutoHidden method also needs to be overridden.

Also note that the root controller is not necessarily UINavigationController or UITabBarController. It may come from a third-party framework, or be custom. For example, QQReader’s root controller, XXXViewController, is custom.

If adaptation ended here, there would be no need for this article. The real trap in adapting QQReader’s Home Indicator had only just begun.

Switching the Reading Page to Night Mode

In my understanding, a normal night mode should use a dedicated skin system. That is simple and reliable. If your project implements night mode this way, you probably will not hit the issue I encountered.

But for historical reasons, some projects may not do this. Instead, they use another approach: add a window that does not respond to events above the current window, then set the opacity of its root view controller’s view to create a night-mode visual effect. This approach may require less work, but it can cause all kinds of problems. It once affected page navigation, and this time it affected Home Indicator adaptation.

Summary

If Home Indicator adaptation is done step by step like this, it usually does not take too much time. Unfortunately, I fell into the night-mode trap at the very beginning and did not expect Home Indicator adaptation to have anything to do with that night-mode implementation, so I spent quite a long time on it.

For a while, I thought this adaptation might be like in-app rating: the client has very weak control. I also trusted Apple’s official opinion quite strongly:

  • The indicator will automatically adjust itself depending on what’s below it, and it’s not something you can directly control. As described in Designing for iPhone X, you generally don’t want to do anything in your app specific to the home indicator

In practice, even after adaptation, the client still has weak control and there remains one limitation: whenever a touch happens inside the reading page, the Home Indicator briefly appears and then slowly disappears. This is likely caused by Apple’s internal implementation, and there may be little we can do. I checked several video and game apps that adapted Home Indicator, and basically all of them have this issue.

Fortunately, gestures do not have much impact. Gesture-based page turning in QQReader’s reading page basically does not make the Home Indicator appear. I suspect this may be related to gesture response priority.

Hopefully there will be a better solution in the future.
