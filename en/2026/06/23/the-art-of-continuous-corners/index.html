The Art of Continuous Corners

Posted on 2026-06-23 | In , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ,
Words count in article: 484k | Reading time ≈ 29:19

Background

After iOS 7, Apple brought the idea of curvature continuity from industrial design into visual design and upgraded the classic rounded app icon outline. Before iOS 7, rounded corners were made only from simple circular arcs. The transition between the straight edge and the arc had an abrupt curvature jump from 0 to a fixed value, which looked visually harsh. The new continuous corner makes the curvature rise smoothly from 0 to the target value and completely solves the awkward transition.

Measured result: size = 320x320, radius = 44, iou = 0.99899. The overlap between the continuous corner and the regular rounded corner is extremely high, reaching 99.899%.

Apple provides a very simple API for continuous corners:

However, Apple does not expose the actual parameters of its Bezier implementation, which makes custom development around continuous corners surprisingly difficult.

So, is this problem solvable? Why do Apple’s continuous corners look so smooth? Can we obtain the parameters and draw the Bezier curve ourselves? This article focuses on that question.

Hypotheses About iOS Continuous Corners

After iOS continuous corners appeared, many technical guesses emerged in the community. Some believed the primitive curve was a superellipse, while others thought it was an Euler spiral. Are these guesses correct?

Is the iOS continuous corner icon a superellipse?

A superellipse is defined as:

When , it becomes a squircle.

As increases, the shape gradually evolves from a circle toward a square, with sharper corners and straighter sides.

Visually, a superellipse is curved everywhere, with no truly flat section. Its curvature changes continuously, and the higher the exponent, the smoother the curvature transition. But Apple’s continuous corner contains at least part of a straight segment, so the conclusion is: the iOS continuous corner icon is not a superellipse.

As a side note, when people mention superellipses, Xiaomi’s 2-million-icon shape is one of the most famous examples. It is essentially a superellipse ( ).

What is easy to overlook is that Xiaomi’s MIUI 12 rendering engine also implemented a “G2 continuous curve, curvature-continuous perfect rounded corner” (although from the plot it looks more like a G3 continuous curve).

Euler spirals are often used in road and railway engineering to ease the curvature change between a straight segment and a circular arc. Their key property is that curvature starts from 0 and increases linearly with arc length. Because of that, as the curve extends, it becomes progressively more curved. This makes it very useful in many applications. When a spiral tail connects to a circle, the whole curve is certainly curvature-continuous.

There are two drawbacks if you use an Euler spiral in this way, i.e. two spiral segments with a circular arc in the middle, to draw a rounded rectangle:

  • the entire curve is curvature-continuous, but the rate of curvature change is not;
  • the curvature increase along an Euler spiral is linear, so although curvature has acceleration, its derivative is constant.

An Euler spiral can also achieve a very close fit to iOS icon corners, but the conclusion here is still: the iOS continuous corner icon is not an Euler spiral.

The Truth About iOS Continuous Corners

Former Apple interaction designer Bas Ording filed patent US9396565 in 2013. The patent is titled “Rendering Boundaries in User Graphical Interface Elements”.

From the patent, we can learn that:

  • an iOS continuous corner is built from two transition curves + one standard circular arc; the curvature is 0 where the transition curves meet the straight edges, then increases smoothly toward the common maximum curvature of the central arc, which keeps the corner fundamentally round;
  • the corner shape can be abstracted with two key control parameters: the corner radius , which determines maximum curvature, and the transition ratio , which determines how much of the corner is used for smoothing;
  • the transition curves on both sides can be implemented with cubic Bezier curves or B-splines, and the full structure can be generated quickly by mirroring.

Reasoning About iOS Continuous Corners

Now that we understand the rough implementation from Apple’s patent, can we simulate it ourselves?

Basics

Curve continuity

In computer graphics, curve continuity is commonly classified into several levels:

  • G0 continuity (position continuity): the endpoints are the same;
  • G1 continuity (tangent continuity): the endpoint tangent directions are the same;
  • G2 continuity (curvature continuity): the endpoint curvature is continuous;
  • G3 continuity (curvature-derivative continuity): the first derivative of curvature is continuous.

Curvature circle & curvature comb

There is also the concept of the curvature circle and the curvature comb. The curvature radius is the reciprocal of curvature. At a point on the curve, you can draw a circle that shares the same tangent and the same curvature direction; this is the curvature circle at that point. There is also a curvature comb: the faster a curve turns, the taller the comb becomes; the slower it turns, the flatter the comb is.

Bias

Bias is a curvature-distribution offset parameter. Changing it does not alter the target curvature and does not change the G-level continuity of the curve. It only controls when the corner starts to bend visibly.

Different bias values correspond to different bending rhythms:

  • bias = 1: the curvature transition is symmetric, and the curvature moves evenly from the straight segment to the circular arc;
  • bias < 1: the curve bends earlier, and curvature rises faster in the first half;
  • bias > 1: the first half stays flatter, and curvature rises sharply later, closer to the arc shape.

The practical differences are easy to see:

  • G2 + bias 1.0: curvature is continuous and the transition is direct;
  • G3 + bias 1.0: curvature changes smoothly and symmetrically;
  • G3 + bias 0.8: the bend starts earlier while keeping a soft transition;
  • G3 + bias 1.6: the front half is flatter and the back half rises quickly toward the arc.

Side Note

During experiments, I found that some cases did not fit perfectly and were not close enough to Apple’s result. That made me suspect that Apple’s patent description and the real implementation might differ somewhat. After all, this patent is already 13 years old, so I measured Apple’s continuous corners again.

The boundary outline of Apple’s continuous corner is sampled, the upper-right contour is extracted and resampled uniformly by arc length s, and the curvature k(s) at each position is computed from three adjacent points. The plateau region in the middle, where curvature is stable, is treated as the standard circular-arc segment, whose curvature is approximately 1/R.

Measuring Apple continuous corners

  • Fixed width and height, varying corner radius

Conclusion:

  1. The peak is not pure random noise: the smaller the radius, the more obvious the peak relative to the plateau; the larger the radius, the closer the peak converges to around 1.14x.
  2. Small radii magnify measurement error. With larger radii, there is still about 17% to 18% overshoot. Apple’s transition curvature is very likely not a simple monotonic rise to the plateau, but instead 0 -> overshoot by about 15%~20% -> fall back to the arc plateau.
  • Fixed corner radius

Conclusion:

  1. When the corner radius is fixed at 44, the peak value stays around 1.20x.

Introducing overshoot

Testing shows that the curvature peak in the transition segment is clearly higher than the reference plateau value, with the peak ratio reaching roughly 1.25x. This proves that Apple’s continuous corner curvature does not increase monotonically to the baseline circular curvature. Instead, it behaves like this: rise from 0, overshoot the baseline slightly, and then fall back to the circular plateau.

So I introduced the concept of overshoot: a bump is added on top of the smooth base curvature function in Core:

  • the bump is 0 at both ends of the transition segment, so it does not break endpoint constraints;
  • the bump reaches a peak in the middle of the transition segment;
  • curvatureOvershoot = 0.18 roughly means the peak curvature is close to 1.18x the arc plateau curvature.

After fitting, overshoot improves the match in some cases, for example: iou 0.9985 -> 0.9994

Continuous Corner Practice

Based on the reasoning above, I built a continuous-corner library with Codex: ContinuousCornerKit (CCK). CCK is a cross-platform continuous-corner geometry and rendering SDK that provides an explainable, debuggable, cacheable, and high-performance rounded-corner system, plus Apple-like curvature fitting.

Git repo: to be provided

From the diagram, the CCKSharedContinuousCorner layer in C++ handles geometry, such as sampling the contour, segment information, and curvature data. These core capabilities can be shared by iOS, Android, and Web. To keep the article focused, I will mainly introduce the iOS side of CCK here. For Android and Web, please refer to the project documentation.

Features

Core

Generates continuous corner paths: input radius, arc angle, G level, bias, and other parameters; output path points, curvature, arc segments, and diagnostic data. In practice, you configure CCKContinuousCornerConfiguration and then use CCKContinuousCornerPathBuilder to generate a Bezier path that can be used directly with CAShapeLayer + path, mask, border layer, and so on.

If you want a stroke animation, you can pass a progress parameter, for example drawing only 50% of the path length:

CCKContinuousCornerConfiguration also exposes more parameters to control corner shape:

  • radius controls the radius
  • circleArcAngleDegrees controls the size of the middle circular arc
  • cornerMask controls which corners are active, with All as the default
  • continuityLevel controls how smooth the transition curve is

GPU Backend

To push performance further, CCK also explores a GPU rendering path that does not rely on CGPath masks, and verifies that the Core Path and GPU output are consistent. This moves part of the drawing work to the GPU. The idea is simple: upload the Core-generated continuous-corner outline to the GPU, then let the shader handle fill, anti-aliasing, and border logic at pixel level.

Its core input looks like this:

The shader handles three main tasks:

  1. Region test Determine whether the current pixel lies inside the closed contour uploaded from Core.

  2. Distance fitting Compute the nearest distance from the current pixel to the contour segments. This is used for edge anti-aliasing and for the border region.

  3. Fill and border composition

    • outside the contour: transparent;
    • inside the contour: fill color;
    • near the contour edge: anti-aliasing based on distance;
    • in the border region: blend the border color and complete the border rendering.
